# First Playtest Protocol — Concept Validation, Not Retention Proof

## Purpose
Test whether a child understands the loop, enjoys the result, and voluntarily asks to continue. Test whether a parent understands the product and trusts its approach. This small study cannot reliably rank long-term retention, revenue or store traction.

Use a first qualitative round of approximately 5–10 parent/child pairs per concept. These are a proposed research sample and not recruited participants. Recruit and communicate with adults, obtain appropriate permission, and do not collect children's names, photos, voices, birthdays, email, school details or contact information. Record broad age band only when genuinely needed. A production research protocol still needs an appropriate privacy review.

## Session script
Tell the parent: “This is an unfinished game. There are no purchases or advertisements in this prototype. We are testing the game, not your child. You may stop at any time.”

Let the child explore before explaining. Observe for a short comfortable session and stop when they wish. Do not use rewards, pressure or daily-streak promises to manufacture engagement.

| Game | First success | Observe | Parent question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pet | Choose a friend, place 3 decorations, try 2 activities, save a memory | Can they find adoption? Move objects? Understand different reactions? Do they choose to redecorate? | What exact new room/pet pack would add value, and why? |
| Garage | Choose parts, finish a course, change a part, try again | Are hold-to-drive controls clear? Do they notice a handling difference? Does a custom track invite another run? | Would a vehicle/track pack add meaningful play, or merely more clutter? |
| Lab | Rotate mirrors, light a lamp, use a hint, solve another route | Can they predict direction? Does help arrive without frustration? Is the effect more engaging than a quiz? | Does this feel like enjoyable discovery, and what evidence would justify an educational claim? |

## Record only what helps a decision
Use a parent-held test code, not a child identity. Keep observations locally with access limited to the research team and a defined deletion date.

Fields: game/build, broad age band if needed, device/browser, order shown, first-success achieved, adult interventions, confusing controls, requests to replay, reasons to stop, parent concerns, exact proposed pack response, defects.

Record “would pay” as an opinion—not a purchase or monetization conversion. Children liking the free prototype does not validate advertising tolerance.

## Compare fairly
Pet targets a younger age band than Garage/Lab. Do not compare raw engagement across these groups as if they were the same market. Counterbalance game order when children try more than one. Note the device and guidance given. Use the same question wording and comparable exposure per concept.

## Suggested qualitative gates (internal proposals, not industry benchmarks)
- Most children can achieve the first success without repeated adult instruction.
- No recurring control problem prevents play.
- Parents can explain what their child did and see a credible reason to allow another session.
- Some children voluntarily change a creation or attempt another puzzle; do not demand longer play.
- Revise confusing onboarding before adding content or purchasing traffic.

## Later retention experiment
After a native closed release and approved measurement design, evaluate D1, D7 and D30 on mature install cohorts, with numerator/denominator and confidence intervals. A browser “visits” number is not a child-level retention metric. Do not use the initial 5–10-family test to claim that the previous retention targets were met.

No advertising experiment runs in this first playtest. Future eligible tests must retain an ad-free group, a fixed observation window, and an explicit stop/revert decision. A schedule alone never justifies increasing ad load.
